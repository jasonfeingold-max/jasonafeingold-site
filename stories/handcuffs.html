Handcuffs

By Jason A. Feingold

The house where the party was that night was attended by the weird, alternative people with lots of piercings in unlikely places. They seemed to all be majoring in philosophy and psychology and wore a lot of black. I wasn’t one of them per se, but I found them better company than the run-of-the-mill undergraduate. They were a hell of a lot more interesting than anyone I was likely to meet at a frat.

The theme of the party was crime – cops and criminals. I’d decided to go the criminal route. I wore my grey-and-white striped shirt with numbers across the left breast written on athletic tape. I’d posed on the cold December pavement so the people throwing the party could make a chalk outline of my body. I’d been upset about how square the tracing looked. I always think I’m taller and thinner than I am.

When she slapped the handcuff on my right wrist, I was a little drunk, but not drunk to the point of incoherence, which is not to say that I wasn’t trying to get hammered. I was leaning on the kitchen counter to keep myself near the large ice bucket that was cooling the font of beer. Unfortunately, the keg we’d all gone in on was filled with Milwaukee’s Best, better known as The Beast. It had been a quantity-over-quality decision. I knew I’d have a vicious hangover the next day, but I was buzzed enough so I didn’t care all that much.

“You got me, copper,” I said, pathetically attempting to imitate Jimmy Cagney and failing. She was unmoved. She wasn’t even looking at me, but I felt that I had earned the right to look her up and down, which I did. She was wearing a black dress that showed lots of leg and lots of cleavage. She was dark-haired, like me, and a little thick, also like me. She was pretty. The other half of the handcuffs was secured to her left wrist. There were a lot of less desirable people to be handcuffed to.

“What are you supposed to be?” I asked.

“Undercover,” she replied.

“I’m Carl,” I said, introducing myself.

“Bethany,” she said, smiling. Her teeth weren’t the straight white American standard-issue teeth. They were yellow and a little shrunken-looking. Maybe she’d been raised in England. They were straight, though. It wasn’t too bad.

“I’d shake hands, but…” I held up my right handcuffed hand. She lifted her left along with me. I shook my wrist.

“But you’re my prisoner,” she finished.

“It kind of works both ways,” I said. “’Every cop is a criminal.’”

“Sympathy For The Devil,” she said, identifying the lyric without a pause. She was quick. “It doesn’t necessarily mean that every criminal is a cop. I could draw you a Venn diagram.”

There was an awkward silence. I could feel her skin radiating heat next to mine.

The party was beginning to fizzle out. The people who hadn’t left were probably paired up in the bedrooms. The living room was all ours.

“What do you do?” she asked. The question took me by surprise. It was a college town. I had pretty much assumed that everybody went to school.

“I go to school,” I said. “Don’t you?”

“Yes, but it’s not what I do. It’s someplace I go. So what do you do?”

“Well, when you put it that way, nothing much. I read books, play darts and pool, drink, and…” I cut myself off.

“And what?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“What they tell me to read. Sometimes I grab books out of the stacks at random. Everything except People Magazine. So what about you?”

“I’m a poet,” she said.

I groaned inwardly. No one had ever said that to me and then read me a decent poem. Granted, people who write poetry, even bad poetry, are better than people who write no poetry at all, but bad poetry is still bad poetry, and I was the epitome of a captive audience.

“Have you published?” I asked, hoping that the question would take some of the wind out of her poetry sails and perhaps spare me a recitation.

“Really? Where?”

“The New Yorker.”

“That’s awesome. I’ve never been chained to a real poet before.”

“Do you have to be published to be a real poet?” she asked, her question pointed all over like a cactus.

“It helps,” I said, not backing down. “I could call myself Emperor of the Milky Way Galaxy, but if no one takes my orders it doesn’t mean a whole lot.” I paused. “Was that the right answer?”

“There was a gap in my soul. I couldn’t fill it, and I couldn’t cross it. Heroin made it easy to ignore it. At least at first.”

“Then I was just another junkie looking for my next fix. Then detox. And here I am.”

“How did you close the gap?” I asked.

“I didn’t. I just accepted it. Just like I accept the gap between people. Speaking of which…”

Bethany produced the handcuff key from somewhere in the pile of clothes gathered around her wrist and removed the cuffs. I took the opportunity to shrug off the rest of my clothing from my arm.

“The gap reopens,” she said sadly.

“Does it have to?” I asked.
